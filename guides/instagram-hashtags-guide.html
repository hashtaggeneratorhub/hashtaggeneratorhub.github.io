Instagram Hashtags: The Complete Guide for 2026

Instagram hashtags have changed a lot since the early days, when thirty tags on every photo was normal. Today the algorithm reads your caption, your image, and your audio as well, so hashtags are one discovery signal among many. They still matter, though, especially for Reels and for accounts that are still growing.

This guide covers how Instagram hashtags work now: how many to use, where to put them, which kinds to mix, and how to tell whether they are actually working. Everything here is practical and free to test on your own account.

How Instagram hashtags work now

When you add a hashtag to a post or Reel, Instagram files it on that tag's page and may show it to people who follow the tag. The tag page shows recent posts and top posts, and Reels have their own tab. Your post competes with every other post using the same tag, which is why very popular tags are hard to rank in.

Instagram also uses hashtags to understand your content's topic. If you post about houseplants with consistent tags, Instagram learns to show your Reels to plant lovers. That learning effect is slow but real, and it rewards consistency over time more than any single viral tag.

How many hashtags to use on Instagram

Instagram allows 30 hashtags per post and 10 per Story. In practice, 5 to 15 well-chosen tags work best for most accounts. Smaller accounts often do better with fewer, more specific tags, because they can actually rank on those tag pages. Large accounts can use broader tags because their engagement pushes them to the top.

For Reels, 3 to 8 tags is a good range. Reels are mainly distributed by the algorithm based on watch time and engagement, so tags play a supporting role. Test 5 tags on your next ten Reels, then test 12, and compare the reach from hashtag pages in your insights.

Where to place hashtags on Instagram

You can put hashtags in the caption or in the first comment. Instagram has confirmed both work for discovery. The caption is the safer choice: it is always attached to the post, while a first comment can be buried under other comments or fail to post. If you prefer a clean caption, put 3 to 5 tags in the caption and the rest in the first comment.

For Stories, use the hashtag sticker or type the tag as text. You can use up to 10, but 1 or 2 is usually enough, and too many stickers make the Story unreadable. Remember that Stories with hashtags can appear on the tag's Story ring, which is a small but real source of new viewers.

Finding hashtags that actually bring reach

Open Instagram search and type a topic. The results show related hashtags and how many posts use each one. Look for tags with tens of thousands to a few million posts: big enough to have an audience, small enough that your post is not buried in seconds. Then open the tag page and check the recent posts. If the top posts are far better than yours, pick a smaller tag.

Check your own insights too. On any post, tap View Insights and look for the impressions from hashtags. If a tag set brings almost no impressions, replace it. If one post got strong hashtag impressions, note which tags it used and reuse the pattern. This feedback loop is more valuable than any hashtag generator.

Key takeaways

  • Use 5 to 15 relevant hashtags per post; fewer, specific tags often beat 30 generic ones.
  • Put hashtags in the caption for reliability, or split them between caption and first comment.
  • Mix tag sizes: a few large, several medium, and a few small niche tags.
  • Check Insights for hashtag impressions and drop tags that bring nothing.

Frequently asked questions

How many hashtags should I use on Instagram Reels?

Three to eight is a good range. Reels are driven mainly by watch time, so tags support discovery rather than carrying it. Test and compare using Insights.

Do hashtags in the first comment still work?

Yes, Instagram treats them the same for discovery. The caption is still the safer place, because a comment can be missed or delayed.

Should I use the same hashtags on every post?

No. Rotate them. Repeating the identical set on every post can look automated and makes it harder to learn which tags actually work.
