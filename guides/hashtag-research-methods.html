Hashtag Research: How to Find the Right Tags for Any Topic

Good hashtags are found, not guessed. Research is the difference between tags that bring the right audience and tags that sit on a post doing nothing. It does not require paid tools: the platforms themselves show you which tags are active, how big they are, and who uses them.

This guide explains four research methods you can use for any topic, from a new product launch to a weekly content series. Used together, they give you a tag list that is specific to your niche and proven by real data.

Method 1: platform search

Start inside the platform where you will post. Type your topic into Instagram, TikTok, or YouTube search and look at the suggested hashtags and the post counts beside them. A tag with 50,000 posts is small and specific; one with 5 million is broad; one with 200 million is a flood. You want a mix, weighted toward the middle.

Open the tag page itself. Look at the top posts: are they from accounts like yours, or from celebrities and brands with millions of followers? Look at the recent posts: are people posting there today, or is the tag dead? A living tag with recent posts from ordinary accounts is a good sign.

Method 2: competitor analysis

Find five accounts in your niche that are doing well, ideally accounts slightly bigger than yours rather than the biggest names. Look at their last twenty posts and note which hashtags repeat. The tags they use again and again are the ones they believe work, and their engagement is the evidence.

Do not copy their list wholesale. Instead, collect the tags that appear across several competitors and check each one yourself. A tag that three successful accounts all use is a strong candidate. A tag that only one uses may be their branded tag, which would be wrong for you to copy.

Method 3: related tags and autocomplete

Platforms suggest related hashtags when you type one into a search box or a caption. These suggestions reveal the vocabulary your audience actually uses. If you type #gardening, the suggestions might include #urbangardening, #vegetablegarden, and #gardenlife, each with its own audience and size.

Autocomplete also reveals how people phrase searches. On TikTok and YouTube, the search bar suggests full phrases, which point to tags worth using. Note the exact spelling people use, including plural forms and local variants, because a tag must match exactly to work.

Method 4: your own analytics

Your analytics are the most honest research source you have. On Instagram, each post shows impressions from hashtags. On TikTok, analytics show traffic from search. On YouTube, the reach report shows traffic from hashtag pages. Compare your last twenty posts and list the tags that appear on the best performers.

Build a simple spreadsheet: tag, times used, average hashtag impressions. After a month you will see clear winners and losers. Retire the losers, keep the winners, and test two or three new tags each week. This is how a generic tag list becomes a personal one.

Key takeaways

  • Research inside the platform itself: search suggestions and post counts are free and accurate.
  • Study five competitors and keep the tags that appear across several of them.
  • Use related-tag suggestions to learn your audience's real vocabulary.
  • Track your own hashtag impressions and retire tags that bring nothing.

Frequently asked questions

Do I need a paid tool for hashtag research?

No. Platform search, competitor posts, and your own analytics cover most research needs. Paid tools help mainly when you manage many accounts or need bulk data.

How often should I research hashtags?

Do a full refresh every two to three months, and test two or three new tags each week. Trends and tag sizes change, so a list from a year ago will be stale.

What is a good hashtag size to target?

Tags with tens of thousands to a few million posts usually work best: big enough to have an audience, small enough that your post stays visible.
